Anatomy of a
Flock camera

There are more than a hundred thousand of these on poles across the United States. This is one of them, at real scale, taken apart one board at a time, with every byte it produces followed to where it ends up.

Built from Flock Safety's own specification sheets and price lists, three independent teardowns, Flock's data-architecture document, FOIA'd audit logs, and court and congressional records. Every figure carries a citation. Where Flock and independent evidence disagree, both are shown.

01 · The pole · installer view

Twelve feet of aluminum, two band clamps, and a cone on the road

A standard Flock install is a dedicated breakaway pole set in dirt or grass by a Flock crew. The camera is banded on at roughly ten feet, aimed diagonally down the lane so the rear plates of departing vehicles pass through a narrow field of view. Nothing runs to the ground: power comes from the panel on top, data leaves over LTE.

Aim the camera

Drag the camera on the pole, or use the sliders. The footprint on the road is the published field of view.

02 · Inside the enclosure

Thirteen parts, front to back

The shell splits on its seam. What comes out is a small Android computer with a phone-class processor, a five-megapixel infrared-optimized sensor, a ring of six 850 nm emitters, a cellular modem, and the radios and sensors that stamp each photo with time and place. Tap any part to isolate it.

03 · Power and cable

What actually plugs in

A stock Falcon has no network cable and is not a PoE device. The only conductors on the pole carry DC from the solar array and the external battery box into the camera's rear connector. Where a city chooses 120 V, a licensed electrician lands mains in a junction box and Flock's AC kit steps it down.

Fiber, SFP modules and PoE enter the picture in one place: Flock Wing, the gateway that ingests a city's existing cameras from the network closet. That path is modeled here too, because it is how a traffic camera you already own becomes a plate reader.

04 · The data · one photograph's journey

From photons to an officer's phone in twelve hops

A car passes. The motion sensor fires, the sensor takes six to twelve stills, the on-board model reads the plate and describes the vehicle, and a few kilobytes leave over LTE. Follow one detection through Flock's cloud, its hot lists, its search network, its audit log, and its deletion timer.
